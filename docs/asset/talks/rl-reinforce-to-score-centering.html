1 · REINFORCE

The hard part: computing $\nabla_\theta J$

Written out, the objective is a sum over every possible completion:

  • $\theta$ appears only in $\pi_\theta(y)$ — how likely each completion is — not in $R(y)$
  • If we could evaluate this sum, we could differentiate it directly…
  • …but there are far too many completions: 150k vocab, 100 tokens → $>10^{{500}}$

Goal: rewrite $\nabla_\theta J$ as an average over samples of something we can differentiate.

1 · REINFORCE

Applying the log-derivative trick

One identity

Chain rule: $\nabla\log\pi=\nabla\pi/\pi$. Multiply both sides by $\pi$.

REINFORCE: sample N, grade them, average “score × reward”. Unbiased but noisy.

A special case of the policy gradient theorem — Sutton, McAllester, Singh & Mansour (NeurIPS 1999); estimator: REINFORCE (Williams, 1992).

2 · Actor-Critic

Why Actor-Critic: what REINFORCE gets wrong

Two problems with REINFORCE

  • Noise: the weight is the raw $R$ — on an always-solved prompt every sample is still pushed up, in a random direction (last slide)
  • Credit assignment: every token gets the same $R$ — good steps and the slip alike

Fix: train a critic $V_\phi(s_t)$ — expected reward from this prefix — and subtract it as a baseline:

Example at “340 +”

$V \approx 0.85$ → pick 68: $A\approx+0.15$, pick 58: $A\approx-0.85$

The slip is pushed down where it happened; an always-solved prompt gives $A\approx0$: no noise. (illustrative numbers)

Why can we subtract $V$?

Fix a prefix $s_t$. There $V(s_t)$ is one number; the next token $a$ is sampled from $\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)$ (we drop $s_t$ below).

Subtracting $V$ changes each sample, not the average: same expected gradient, less noise. Needs $a\sim\pi$ itself — remember this for part 6.

Cost

For LLMs the critic is usually as big as the policy, and hard to train well.

3 · TRPO

From Actor-Critic to TRPO: limit the step in policy space

Why move on from Actor-Critic?

  • The PG formula is on-policy: $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[\cdots]$ needs samples from the current policy. After one step the batch is stale → sample, step once, throw away
  • Rollouts are expensive → reuse the batch via importance sampling, weighting by $r=\pi/\mu$. But this is only accurate while $\pi$ stays near $\mu$
  • And a small change in $\theta$ can be a big change in $\pi$ → bound the step in policy space (KL)

$\mu$: the policy that generated the rollout. At the first update $\pi=\mu$, so $r$ should be exactly 1 — remember this for part 5.

Downsides of TRPO

  • Fisher-vector products: second-order information, extra backward passes
  • Conjugate gradient: ~10 iterations to approximate $F^{{-1}}g$, each one an FVP
  • Line search: shrink the step until the KL constraint holds and the loss improves — more forward passes
  • Its own optimizer, not Adam — awkward with huge sharded models

PPO’s question: can plain Adam + one simple trick get most of this?

5 · Train–Inference Mismatch

Same weights, same tokens — should the log-probs match?

  • RL runs on two engines: the trainer (TorchTitan / Megatron) recomputes $\log\pi$, the generator (vLLM / SGLang) reports $\log\mu$
  • Weights are bit-identical (same tensor, copied across); the tokens are the same tokens
  • So at the first update we expect $r=\exp(\log\pi-\log\mu)=1$ exactly

$r$ is an exponential of a difference: an error of 0.1 in log-prob is ~10% in $r$ — and PPO’s clip is only ±0.2. In MoE RL, router mismatch alone can collapse training (R3).

They don’t match. Why can two systems with the same weights and the same tokens produce different log-probs? The answer is three layers down:

② Kernel: the batch shape picks the summation order

① Floating point→② Kernel→③ Engine

Every linear layer is a matmul; every output number is one big sum:

Qwen3.5-9B: 4096 additions per output number.

  • A GPU (~150 SMs) doesn’t add left to right — it partitions the sum to keep busy
  • Large batch: many output tiles, GPU already full → one worker walks $k=0\ldots4095$ → a chain
  • Small batch: too few tiles, SMs idle → split K across workers, add the partials afterwards → a tree

Both are correct; neither is more correct. A heuristic picks one at launch time, from the shapes. So the reduction order is a property of the shape, not of the math.

Why mismatch hurts: drift

Notation. The update from a rollout sums its token scores, each weighted by the rollout’s reward (or advantage) $R$. Look at one token $y_t$ given its prefix $y_{{<t}}$; $v$ indexes the vocabulary. $\pi_v$ and $\mu_v$ are the next-token probabilities of $v$ under the trainer $\pi$ and the sampler $\mu$; $s_v=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_v$ is its score; $\bar s=\sum_v\mu_v s_v$ is the expected score under the sampler.

Constant reward $+1$: no signal, so there should be no learning. On-policy that holds at every prefix:

Under mismatch the token is sampled from $\mu$ but the score goes through $\pi$, so $\bar s=\mathbb{E}_\mu[s_{{y_t}}]\neq0$. With $\mathbb{E}[AB]=\mathbb{E}[A]\,\mathbb{E}[B]+\mathrm{{Cov}}(A,B)$:

Only the signal sees which token led to which reward. The drift depends on the rewards only through their mean.

Drift = distillation toward the sampler

  • $\bar s$ is minus the gradient of the cross-entropy with $\mu$ as the teacher → drift = SFT on the sampler’s distribution, at every prefix, scaled by $\mathbb{E}_\mu[R]$
  • With a constant reward ($R=1$), minimizing that loss trains $\pi$ to imitate $\mu$: off-policy policy gradient is supervised distillation of the trainer toward the sampler. It pulls $\pi$ toward whatever $\mu$ does — including every quirk of its numerics or staleness.

On-policy ($\mu=\pi$): $\bar s=0$, no drift. Group centering shrinks it but can’t remove it: the expected advantage at a prefix isn’t 0.

6 · Score Centering

Score centering: subtract the expected score

  • Drift is cancelled exactly, at every prefix
  • At each prefix, same as on-policy except the covariance is under $\mu$ (the token and the rest of the rollout), not $\pi$. Prefixes are also visited under $\mu$.
  • No mismatch ($\pi=\mu$) → $\bar s=0$ exactly → SC changes nothing, sample by sample (top-$k$: $\rho=1$, all coefficients 0)

$y_t$: sampled token · $s_v=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_v$: score of token $v$ (through the trainer) · $\pi_v$ / $\mu_v$: trainer / sampler prob. · $\bar s$: expected score under the sampler · $\mathrm{{Cov}}_\mu$: covariance with $y_t$ and the rest of the rollout drawn from $\mu$

Working example: two tokens, a sampler that disagrees

One prefix, two possible next tokens. The reward is always 1, so the correct update is 0.

tokentrainer $\pi$sampler $\mu$score $s$IS: ×$\pi/\mu$SC: $s-\bar s$
A0.50.9$+u$×0.56$+0.2u$
B0.50.1$-u$×5$-1.8u$

$s_B=-s_A$ because $\pi_A s_A+\pi_B s_B=\nabla(\pi_A+\pi_B)=0$; write $u=s_A$. Expected score under the sampler: $\bar s=0.9u-0.1u=0.8u$.

Two ways to remove the $0.8u$

  • IS reweights each sample. B is rare (10%), so when it shows up it counts 5×: right on average, but big kicks
  • SC shifts every sample by the same $\bar s=0.8u$: no sample is amplified

A batch of 10 tokens; B shows up 0, 1 or 2 times. The update:

0 (35%)$+1.0u$$+0.56u$$+0.56u$$+0.2u$

expected$+0.8u$$0$$+0.3u$$0$
variance0.362.780.590.36
  • No fix: always pushes toward A, the sampler’s favorite → drift = imitate $\mu$
  • Exact IS: 0 on average, but swings $\pm0.56u$ (variance 7.7× SC)
  • TIS: clipping calms the swings, but drift leaks back ($+0.3u$)
  • SC: 0 on average, with the same spread as no fix, just shifted to 0

Variance per token, in units of $u^2$. A constant reward isolates the drift. With real rewards, SC’s signal is $\mathrm{{Cov}}_\mu$ while exact IS’s is $\mathrm{{Cov}}_\pi$: SC takes a little bias for much less variance.

Implementation: sampler top-$k$ + one scalar loss

$\bar s$ needs the sampler’s full distribution: 152K vocab × fp32 = 608 KB per token → 1024 rollouts × 32K tokens = 20 TB. So log only the sampler’s top-$k$ ($k=128$) as the head $H$, and fill the tail with the trainer, rescaled:

Same $\bar s$ as before; only the tail is approximated, $\mu_v\approx\rho\,\pi_v$ — and it collapses to only $k$ terms:

$k=128$ and even $k=32$ match full SC; <1% wall-clock overhead. vLLM / SGLang already return top-$k$ logprobs.

As a scalar loss (sg = stop-gradient):

def sc_loss(train_logp, samp_logp, topk_ids, tok, adv): head = train_logp[topk_ids] rho = (1 - exp(samp_logp).sum()) / (1 - exp(head).sum()) coef = exp(samp_logp) - rho * exp(head) corr = (stop_gradient(coef) * head).sum() return -adv * (train_logp[tok] - corr)

Adapted from the paper’s JAX snippet (Sec. 4.1). Differentiating gives $A\,(s_{y_t}-\bar s)$. $\pi_v$ / $\mu_v$: trainer / sampler prob. of token $v$ · $y_t$: sampled token · $A$: advantage · $H$: sampler’s top-$k$ tokens · sg: stop-gradient.

6 · Score Centering

Why only LLMs?

Subtracting the expected score is an obvious idea — why wasn’t it used before?

Classical off-policy RL (robotics)

  • $s$: state, $a$: action — continuous
  • On-policy it’s 0; off-policy it’s an intractable integral
  • → everyone uses importance sampling instead

LLMs under train–inference mismatch

  • $\mathcal V$: vocabulary; $\mu_k,\pi_k$: sampler / trainer prob. of token $k$
  • Nonzero: $\mu\neq\pi$ (numerics, staleness)
  • Computable exactly: a finite sum over next-token logprobs (top-$k$=128 is enough)

“RL training of LLMs under TIM is unusual in that the expected score is both nonzero and computable exactly.” — Marek & Ryabinin (2026)

Async RL — and SC + async is unexplored

Rollouts ∥ training · buffer bounds off-policyness · TorchStore weight sync

In-place update: pause → load $w_{{k+1}}$ → resume, KV kept → one sequence, two weight versions

Future research: SC + async RL has not been explored

Theory. Signal is $\mathrm{{Cov}}_\mu$, not $\mathrm{{Cov}}_\pi$ — under staleness, a bias nobody has bounded.

Practice. Only test: 0.6B, sampler synced every 64 steps — no real async pipeline; SC alone < SC + TIS.

Key questions. ① How many async (off-policy) steps can SC tolerate? ② Can SC replace IS in async RL — removing the bias that clipped IS introduces?
